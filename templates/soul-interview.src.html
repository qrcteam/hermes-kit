The SOUL Interview

Forty-five minutes of questions whose answers become someone's assistant. You ask, they talk, the recorder runs. This is the actual work — the install is plumbing, an afternoon of mostly waiting for Docker. This is what separates an assistant that knows someone from a chatbot wearing their name.

Length
~45 min
Questions
27
Must-ask
6
Capture
Recorded

Before you sit down

  • 1
    Sit with them. A phone call is fine. Texting is not. You need the tangents — the aside they add after answering is usually better than the answer.
  • 2
    Ask permission, then start recording. A voice memo on the table is enough. You are not transcribing while they talk — you are listening for the sharp specific line you will quote later.
  • 3
    Don't ask all 27. Ask what fits the person in front of you. A short honest briefing beats a padded one, and question 4 matters more than 12 through 18 combined.
  • 4
    Write it up afterwards, in your own prose. Not a Q&A dump — briefing notes for a new chief of staff who starts Monday. Quote them directly where they said it well; their words beat your summary every time.

The two rules that make it good

Specific beats complete

Five sharp facts outperform forty vague ones.

“Mornings are calls and admin, afternoons are for making things.” “Values work–life balance.”

True beats flattering

The assistant calibrates every suggestion off what it believes. If it thinks they're comfortable, it will propose expensive things. Write what's true, and date it.

“Revenue is $0/mo.” “Growing steadily.”

If you only get fifteen minutes

These six carry most of the weight. Ask them in this order and you have a usable briefing even if the conversation ends early.

  1. 04If this thing works perfectly, what does it do for you?
  2. 07What have you already decided not to do?
  3. 12When an assistant annoys you, what did it just do?
  4. 20What does your day shape look like?
  5. 24What must it never, ever do?
  6. 26What have you learned the hard way?

Identity

~6 min · Q1–4

Who am I talking to?

01

What do you actually do all day?

Not their title. What the hours go into.

02

How would you describe your own working style?

Push for their phrase, not a category.

From a real briefing
  • “Self-described: messy creative. The systems around him exist to organize without adding friction.”

That one line permanently changes how the assistant proposes things.

03

Where are you, and what's your timezone?

Drives every “today”, every “tomorrow”, and the hour their daily digest lands. Get it exactly right.

04
Most important question in the interview

If this thing works perfectly, what does it do for you?

One sentence. It goes at the top of the file and shapes everything else. Don't accept “help me be organised” — ask what being disorganised actually cost them this week.

Two real answers
  • “I want to know what I was working on.” — Doug
  • “I want ideas caught before they evaporate.” — Mazíx

These lead to two very different assistants.

Mission

~8 min · Q5–8

What are they pushing at?

05

What are you trying to make happen in the next few months?

With a number and a date, if there is one.

06

What's the one thing that, if it slipped, would matter most?

07
The section people skip

What have you already decided not to do?

The most valuable answers in the whole interview. Every “no” here is a suggestion the assistant won't waste their time with, and a debate they won't have to have twice.

Real answers
  • “No paid ads until three case studies exist.”
  • “No new features unless a paying client needs them.”
  • “The old ten-clients-in-six-weeks target is dead.”
08

What does everyone keep telling you to do that you're deliberately not doing?

Question 7 asked sideways. It often gets the better answer — ask it even if they already answered 7.

Work

~6 min · Q9–11

The situation, honestly.

Skip this whole block if the assistant is for personal life only.
09

What do you sell, and how does money actually work?

If prices change, ask where the real answer lives. A rule like “read the file, never quote from memory” belongs in the briefing verbatim.

10

Where do things actually stand right now — revenue, costs, runway?

Be blunt with them about why you're asking: an assistant that thinks they're flush will suggest expensive things.

11

Who else is involved?

“Strictly solo — there is nobody to delegate to” changes every proposal the assistant makes.

Voice

~9 min · Q12–18

How to talk to them. Get this wrong and they'll stop using it inside a week.

12
Ask it exactly like this

When an assistant annoys you, what did it just do?

The best question in this section. Asking it the abstract way (“what tone do you prefer?”) gets you nothing.

Answers that became rules
  • “Never ask me to confirm an obvious next step.”
  • “One question at a time. Don't stack them.”
  • “Short by default. No bullet walls.”
13

Long or short? Answer first, or reasoning first?

14

Do you want to be told when you're wrong?

Some people genuinely don't. Believe them.

15

Casual or formal?

Match their register. If they say “dude” during the interview, write that down.

16

You're often away from your phone — should it acknowledge a message before it starts working?

Silence reads as broken to most people.

17

Do you send voice notes? And do you want spoken replies back?

Two separate questions — ask both. Most people send voice constantly once they realise they can. Far fewer want audio coming back. Don't assume the answer to one is the answer to the other.

18

Will it ever draft anything you send to someone else?

If yes: spelling, case, banned punctuation, and the words they hate.

Rhythm

~7 min · Q19–23

When to speak, and when to shut up.

19

When are you sharpest?

The block to protect.

20
Best value per minute in the interview

What does your day shape look like?

No other single line does more for a daily digest.

A real answer
  • “Mornings are calls and admin; afternoons and evenings are creative work.”

The assistant then puts phone-and-form tasks in the morning and judgment tasks in the afternoon.

21

When should it absolutely not ping you?

Quiet hours. Non-negotiable, and the fastest way to get an assistant muted forever.

22

Is there one habit that, if it slips, everything slips?

If they have one, that's the single nudge worth making. Write it down as exactly that.

23

What do you do when it's going badly?

So the assistant can say “go for a walk” instead of offering another tactic.

A real answer
  • “Recovery is getting out of the house. If he's been at the screen all day and things are grinding, that's the suggestion — not another tactic.”

Hard nos

~3 min · Q24

The rules with consequences attached.

24
Push for the consequence, not the rule

What must it never, ever do?

A rule with evidence attached gets followed — and the assistant generalises correctly to the cases you didn't list. So don't stop at the rule. Ask what happened.

A real answer, with its receipt
  • “Never fetch a /go/ link — every request records a real click and corrupts his prospect stats. Four false opens already on record.”

The receipt is what makes it stick.

Always include these, whatever they say
  • Never paste secrets into chat, never commit personal information.
  • Never claim a fact they haven't shared — say “I don't know” instead.
  • Never guarantee an outcome on their behalf.

People & history

~6 min · Q25–27

The names it will hear, and the lessons it should already know.

25

Who will you mention that it needs to know instantly?

Name, relationship, and the one thing that matters — not a biography.

26
Only exists if you ask

What have you learned the hard way?

Often the best content in the whole file. Get the cost attached — the number is what makes it land.

A real answer
  • “13 audits, 0 closes on the ownership pitch. Ownership was never why anyone said yes.”
27

What decisions are settled that you don't want reopened?

Date them. It stops the assistant relitigating the same question every few weeks.

After the interview

The recording was scaffolding. The write-up is the product.

Transcribe on your own machine. This recording is the most personal artifact in the kit — it never goes to a cloud transcriber.

ffmpeg -i interview.m4a -ar 16000 -ac 1 /tmp/interview.wav
whisper-cli -m ~/.cache/whisper/ggml-small.en.bin -f /tmp/interview.wav -nt > interview.txt

No whisper-cli? brew install whisper-cpp, then download the small.en model once.

Raw audio without a transcript is a last resort — you will never scrub through 45 minutes again. The transcript is what you actually write from. Once the write-up is done, delete the audio.

The write-up checklist

  • Every placeholder and comment removed. A placeholder left in is something the assistant will confidently reason from as though it were true.
  • Unknowns marked ⟨ASK⟩, not guessed. A blank the assistant asks about beats a plausible invention.
  • Volatile facts dated, so a stale one is visibly stale.
  • Their words quoted where they said it well.
  • Read it aloud. If a section sounds like a form, rewrite it as a sentence.
  • Show it to them. They'll correct one thing you got importantly wrong. They always do. Worth ten minutes.

Then restart the assistant and test it with something only a well-briefed one could get right — “should I call Scott today or tomorrow?” A generic answer means the briefing is thin.

Round two is the good one

The first version is always half-blind: it captures what someone says about themselves before the assistant has lived with them. Two to three weeks of real use is the best interview prep that exists. Book round two then — about 20 minutes, and different questions:

  1. What did it get wrong about you? Every irritation is a missing or wrong line.
  2. Which blanks can we now fill?
  3. What dated facts have changed? Revenue, targets, decisions — update and re-date.
  4. What rule do you wish it had? They'll have one by now. Get the consequence attached.

Revise, don't rewrite. Keep every line that use has proven true; fix what use disproved. After round two, a yearly pass is usually enough.

A note on decay. This briefing is the file that goes stale fastest, because it holds exactly the facts that change. A stale one is worse than a short one, because the assistant trusts it completely. Everything else grows on its own — this file only changes if a human changes it.
