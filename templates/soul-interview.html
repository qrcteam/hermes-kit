The SOUL Interview

Your SOUL file teaches Hermes how to work with you — what matters right now, how you think, how you want to be spoken to, and what it should never assume. This page is the conversation that produces it.

You don't need perfect answers. Specific, honest ones are far more useful than polished ones.

Length

Prompts

Best done
Out loud

Would rather not do the long version? The 15-minute self-serve route hands a prompt to the ChatGPT or Claude you already use — it interviews you and produces a draft you can send straight on. Same file at the end; this page just goes deeper and gets it in your own words.

What to expect

  • 1
    Answer out loud. Have someone ask you these, or talk into a recorder on your own. Speaking gets you the tangents, and the aside you add after answering is usually better than the answer.
  • 2
    Record it. A voice memo on the table is enough. Nobody should be typing while you talk. The recording is transcribed later, on your own machine — it never goes to a cloud service.
  • 3
    These are 27 prompts, not 27 required answers. It's a question bank. Follow the conversation, skip anything that doesn't apply to you, and go deeper wherever an answer feels important. A short honest briefing beats a padded one.
  • 4
    What comes out is a short file, not a transcript. Afterwards the conversation becomes SOUL.md — a page or two of durable statements Hermes can reason from. Your exact words get quoted where you said something well.

Two things worth knowing before you start

Specific beats comprehensive

You don't need to document your whole life. A handful of specific truths are worth far more to Hermes than a long list of vague preferences.

“I don't schedule calls after 4pm because evenings are family time.” “I value work–life balance.”

Describe reality, not the ideal version

Hermes makes decisions based on what you tell it. Describe how things actually work today, even when that's different from how you'd like them to work. If a fact changes often — revenue, deadlines, priorities — note the date.

“I usually don't start focused work until after lunch.” “I try to protect my mornings for deep work.”

Identity

~6 min · Q1–4

Help Hermes understand your world. Who you are, where your time actually goes, how you work, and what you ultimately want it to help with.

01

What does a normal day actually involve for you?

Follow up with

Forget the job title for a second — where does the time really go?

02

When you're working well, what does that tend to look like?

Why this mattersHermes suggests work differently depending on how you actually operate. Your own phrasing is more useful here than a category.

Follow up with

Structured, improvisational, obsessive, easily distracted, somewhere in between? Use your own words.

From a real briefing
  • “Self-described messy creative. The systems around me exist to organize without adding friction.”

One line like that permanently changes how Hermes proposes things.

03

Where are you, and what timezone are you in?

Why this mattersThis drives every “today”, every “tomorrow”, and the hour your daily digest arrives. Worth getting exactly right.

04
The one that shapes everything else

If this works perfectly, what does it do for you?

Why this mattersYour answer goes at the top of the file, and every other answer gets read in light of it. One sentence is plenty.

If the answer comes out vague

“Help me get organised” is hard to act on. What did being disorganised actually cost you this week?

Two real answers
  • “I want to know what I was working on.” — Doug
  • “I want ideas caught before they evaporate.” — Mazíx

Those two produce very different assistants.

Mission

~8 min · Q5–8

What matters right now? What you're trying to make happen, what can't slip, and what you've deliberately decided not to pursue.

05

What are you trying to make happen over the next few months?

Follow up with

If there's a number, a milestone, or a date attached to it, include that too.

06

Of everything you're working on, what can least afford to slip?

07
Easy to skip, hard to replace

What have you already decided not to do?

Why this mattersEvery “no” here is a suggestion Hermes won't waste your time with, and an argument you won't have to have twice.

Real answers
  • “No paid ads until three case studies exist.”
  • “No new features unless a paying client needs them.”
  • “The old ten-clients-in-six-weeks target is dead.”
08

What does everyone keep telling you to do that you're deliberately not doing?

Why this mattersSame ground as the last question from a different angle, and it often gets the better answer. Worth answering even if you think you've covered it.

Work

~6 min · Q9–11

Give Hermes the real-world context. How the work actually functions, what resources genuinely exist, and who else is involved.

Skip this whole section if your Hermes is for personal life only. Nothing later depends on it.
09

What do you sell, and how does the money actually work?

Follow up with

If your prices change, where does the real answer live? A rule like “read the file, never quote from memory” is worth saying out loud.

10

Where do things actually stand right now — revenue, costs, runway?

Why this mattersHermes calibrates what it suggests off this. An assistant that thinks you're flush will propose expensive things.

11

Who else is involved?

A real answer
  • “Strictly solo — there is nobody to delegate to.”

That single line changes every proposal Hermes makes.

Voice

~9 min · Q12–18

How should Hermes talk to you? What feels useful, what feels irritating, and how direct, brief, or proactive it should be. Get this wrong and you'll stop using it inside a week.

12
Ask it exactly like this

When an assistant annoys you, what did it just do?

Why this mattersConcrete irritations turn straight into rules. The abstract version — “what tone do you prefer?” — gets nothing useful.

Try thinking of

AI assistants, apps, coworkers, even emails that make you roll your eyes. What was the behaviour that did it?

Answers that became rules
  • “Never ask me to confirm an obvious next step.”
  • “One question at a time. Don't stack them.”
  • “Short by default. No bullet walls.”
13

How do you like answers delivered?

For example

Short by default or detailed? Answer first or explanation first? Bullets or prose?

14

If Hermes thinks you're mistaken or overlooking something, how should it handle that?

Why this mattersSome people want to be challenged directly, some genuinely don't, and plenty want it only on things that matter. All of those are fine — just say which.

15

Casual or formal?

Follow up with

Talk the way you'd actually talk. If “dude” is in your vocabulary, that's worth knowing.

16

When you're away from your phone, should Hermes acknowledge a message before it starts working?

Why this mattersSilence reads as broken to most people.

17

Do you send voice notes? And do you want spoken replies back?

Why this mattersThese are two separate questions. Most people send voice constantly once they realise they can; far fewer want audio coming back.

18

Will Hermes ever draft something you send to someone else?

If yes

Spelling, capitalisation, punctuation you don't want anywhere near your name, and the words you can't stand.

Rhythm

~7 min · Q19–23

When should Hermes help, and when should it leave you alone? Your energy, your schedule, your routines, your quiet hours, and the nudges that actually help.

19

When are you sharpest?

Why this mattersThat's the block worth protecting.

20
The most useful minute you'll spend

What shape does your day usually take?

Why this mattersNothing else you say does more for your daily digest.

A real answer
  • “Mornings are calls and admin; afternoons and evenings are creative work.”

Hermes then puts phone-and-form tasks in the morning and judgment tasks in the afternoon.

21

When should it absolutely not ping you?

Why this mattersQuiet hours are non-negotiable, and getting them wrong is the fastest way to mute an assistant forever.

22

Is there one habit that, when it slips, everything else slips?

Why this mattersIf you have one, that's the single nudge worth Hermes making.

23

What do you do when it's going badly?

Why this mattersSo Hermes can suggest the thing that actually helps, instead of handing you another tactic.

A real answer
  • “Recovery is getting out of the house. If I've been at the screen all day and things are grinding, that's the suggestion — not another tactic.”

Boundaries

~3 min · Q24

Set the hard limits now. The things Hermes must never do, assume, expose, promise, or decide on your behalf.

24
Say what could go wrong, not just the rule

What should Hermes never do without asking you first?

Why this mattersA rule is much easier for Hermes to apply correctly when it understands what can go wrong if it ignores it — and it will then generalise sensibly to the cases you didn't think to list.

Try asking

Is there anything it should never send, publish, delete, buy, change, or share on your behalf?

Are there systems, files, people, or subjects it should treat as especially sensitive?

Has an assistant or automation crossed a line on you before? What happened?

A real answer, with the reason attached
  • “Never fetch a /go/ link — every request records a real click and corrupts my prospect stats. Four false opens already on record.”

The consequence is what makes the rule stick.

Already built in — you don't need to ask for these
  • It never pastes secrets into chat or commits personal information.
  • It never claims a fact you haven't shared — it says “I don't know” instead.
  • It never guarantees an outcome on your behalf.

People & history

~6 min · Q25–27

Give Hermes the context you shouldn't have to explain twice. The people you mention often, the lessons that cost you something, and the decisions that are already settled.

25

Who are the people you're likely to mention often?

Keep it to three thingsTheir name, how they relate to you, and the one thing Hermes needs to know. Not a biography — and nothing sensitive that isn't necessary.

26
This one only exists if someone asks

What have you learned the hard way that Hermes shouldn't make you learn again?

Why this mattersThis is often the best content in the whole file.

Follow up with

Was there a decision, an experiment, an expense, or a repeated pattern behind it? The specifics are what let Hermes generalise the lesson.

A real answer
  • “13 audits, 0 closes on the ownership pitch. Ownership was never why anyone said yes.”
27

What have you already decided that you don't want Hermes repeatedly suggesting or debating?

Follow up with

When did you decide it, and what would have to change before it's worth reconsidering?

Why this mattersThat gives Hermes both a boundary and the condition for reopening it, so it stops relitigating the same question every few weeks.

That's enough

The goal was never to capture everything about you. It's to give Hermes enough true context to stop behaving like a stranger.

For the person setting this up

Everything below is the mechanical part: turning the recording into the file Hermes actually reads.

1 · Transcribe it on your own machine

This recording is the most personal artifact in the kit. It never goes to a cloud transcriber.

ffmpeg -i interview.m4a -ar 16000 -ac 1 /tmp/interview.wav
whisper-cli -m ~/.cache/whisper/ggml-small.en.bin -f /tmp/interview.wav -nt > interview.txt

No whisper-cli? brew install whisper-cpp, then download the small.en model once.

Raw audio without a transcript is a last resort — nobody scrubs through 45 minutes twice. The transcript is what you write from. Once the write-up is done, delete the audio.

2 · Turn it into SOUL.md, not a transcript

The file should hold concise, durable statements Hermes can reason from. Strip the conversational filler and keep the operating truth. Quote directly only where the exact wording carries meaning.

What they said

“I don't know, mornings are kind of chaotic because I'm usually doing calls and emails and then after lunch I finally get into actual design stuff.”

What goes in SOUL.md

Mornings are generally for calls and admin. Focused creative work usually happens after lunch. Protect afternoons from unnecessary meetings.

3 · Separate what lasts from what expires

Durable — write plainly
  • Communication preferences
  • Working style
  • Boundaries
  • Recurring rhythms
  • Important relationships
  • Lessons learned
Temporary — add a date
  • Revenue and costs
  • Current goals and deadlines
  • Team composition
  • Active projects
  • Current constraints

Mark temporary facts with an “as of” date. Otherwise a six-month-old situation quietly becomes Hermes's permanent picture of the world.

4 · Leave the unknowns visible

Never infer an answer just because the transcript makes one seem plausible. If something important didn't come up, write it as a question:

⟨ASK: What time should quiet hours begin?⟩

Hermes should be able to tell the difference between something the person said, something you inferred, and something nobody knows yet. Where you can, keep inferred personal facts out of the file entirely.

5 · Have them read it

Before Hermes starts relying on the file, the person it describes should read it once. Ask them to look for:

  • Something that isn't true.
  • Something technically true but misleading.
  • A boundary that's missing.
  • A preference stated too strongly. Hermes will apply it more literally than you'd expect.
  • A temporary fact with no date on it.
  • Anything they'd be uncomfortable having Hermes use as context.

Then restart Hermes and test it with something only a well-briefed assistant could get right — “should I call Scott today or tomorrow?” A generic answer means the file is still thin.

The useful version comes after you've used it

The first file is always half-blind: it captures what someone says about themselves before Hermes has lived with them. Two to three weeks of real use is the best interview prep that exists. Book twenty minutes then, and ask a different set of questions:

  1. What has Hermes misunderstood about you?
  2. What has it done that annoyed you? Every irritation is a missing or wrong line.
  3. What does it keep asking that it should already know?
  4. What assumptions has it made incorrectly?
  5. Which facts or priorities have changed? Update them and re-date them.
  6. What new rule would make it noticeably better? There'll be one by now.

Update the existing file rather than regenerating it. Keep every line that real use has proven true, and fix what it disproved. Real behaviour is better calibration data than hypothetical preferences. After this pass, a yearly review is usually enough.

A note on decay. This is the file that goes stale fastest, because it holds exactly the facts that change. A stale one is worse than a short one, because Hermes trusts it completely. Everything else grows on its own — this file only changes if a human changes it.
